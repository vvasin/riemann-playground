Prime numbers and the counting function π(x)

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that is divisible only by 1 and by itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …

Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from the order of the factors. For example, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3, and no other set of primes multiplies to 12. This fact is used in step 4.

Primes become rarer as numbers get larger, and they do not follow a simple pattern. There are 25 primes up to 100, but only 16 between 1001 and 1100. After the prime 1129, the next prime is 1151.

To study how primes are spread out, we count them. π(x), read “pi of x”, is the number of primes less than or equal to x. For example, π(10) = 4, because the primes up to 10 are 2, 3, 5 and 7. Here π is only the name of this function. It has nothing to do with the number 3.14159…

The question behind this page: how accurately can π(x) be predicted without listing the primes?
