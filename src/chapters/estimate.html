Estimating how many primes there are

Around 1800, Gauss noticed from tables of primes that near a number n, roughly one number in every ln n is prime. (ln is the natural logarithm.) Near 1000, ln n ≈ 6.9, so about one number in 7 is prime. Near 1 000 000, about one in 14 is.

If a number n is prime with “probability” about 1/ln n, then the expected number of primes up to x is about

1/ln 2 + 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 4 + … + 1/ln x

A smooth version of this sum is called the logarithmic integral, li(x). (Precisely, li(x) is the area under the curve 1/ln t from t = 0 to t = x. At t = 1 the curve goes to −∞ from the left and to +∞ from the right. The area is measured symmetrically on both sides of t = 1, so that these two infinite parts cancel.) A simpler but less accurate estimate is x / ln x.

The prime number theorem, proved in 1896, says that the ratio π(x) / li(x) gets closer and closer to 1 as x grows. So the estimate is right in relative terms.

The difference li(x) − π(x), however, does not stay bounded. It changes sign infinitely often (see the note under the graph), and its size gets arbitrarily large. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a precise limit on how large: about √x, up to a factor of ln x (step 10).

Key point: the difference between π(x) and li(x) is the error of the estimate. The rest of the page explains what controls this error.
