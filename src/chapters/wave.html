One zero produces one wave

To connect the zeros with primes we use a counting function that is a little different from π(x). ψ(x), read “psi of x”, is a staircase that steps up at every prime and every power of a prime. The step at a power of p has height ln p:

x:    2    3    4=2²  5    7    8=2³  9=3² …
step: ln2 ln3 ln2    ln5 ln7 ln2    ln3   …

The step heights ln p compensate for primes becoming rarer (about one in ln n, step 2), so ψ(x) stays close to x. ψ contains the same information as π: either can be computed from the other. It is used because its formula is simpler.

Riemann (1859) and von Mangoldt (1895) proved an exact formula for ψ:

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − ln(2π) − ½·ln(1 − 1/x²)The sum Σ runs over all nontrivial zeros ρ. Valid for x > 1. At a step, the formula gives the midpoint of the step.

Except for the sum over zeros, the right side is a smooth curve. So every step of the staircase is produced by the zeros.

Why each zero gives a wave

For a zero ρ = β + iγ, Euler's formula gives

xρ = xβ · xiγ = xβ · (cos(γ ln x) + i sin(γ ln x))

Adding the term of the mirror zero β − iγ cancels the imaginary parts. Together the pair contributes the real function

−2·xβ · (β cos(γ ln x) + γ sin(γ ln x)) / (β² + γ²)

This is a wave. Its two numbers play different roles:

  • The height γ sets how fast it oscillates. The oscillation is regular in ln x, not in x.
  • The real part β sets how fast its size grows: in proportion to xβ. For β = ½ the size grows like √x.
Key point: each pair of zeros adds one wave to the prime staircase. The height of the zero gives the frequency; the real part gives the growth rate.
