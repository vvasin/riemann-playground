The Riemann hypothesis

Every nontrivial zero of the zeta function has real part ½.

In the picture of step 5: all nontrivial zeros lie on the vertical line σ = ½, called the critical line.

What is known

  • All nontrivial zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. That none lie on the edge σ = 1 is what proves the prime number theorem (1896).
  • Infinitely many zeros lie on the line (Hardy, 1914), and more than 40% of all of them do (Conrey, 1989).
  • The first 10 trillion zeros have been computed, and all are on the line.
  • Checking any finite number of zeros cannot settle a statement about infinitely many. For this reason the hypothesis was one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute.

The proof

On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis written in Lean. Lean is a programming language for proofs: a program checks every logical step, so a proof it accepts contains no gap or error. The proof is about 17 million lines long.

On 21 November 2026, the Lean and Mathlib communities published a joint statement. Several independent checking programs accepted the proof. They also confirmed that the theorem it proves is exactly the statement above, as written in Mathlib, Lean's shared library of mathematics. The statement says they have full confidence that the proof is correct.

A computer check confirms that the proof is correct. It does not explain its ideas. Mathematicians are now studying the proof to understand how it works. The Clay Mathematics Institute has its own rules for awarding its prize, and that process has not finished.

Why ½

Zeros come in mirror pairs with real parts β and 1 − β (step 5). If one zero has β > ½, its partner has β < ½, and vice versa. So the largest real part among all zeros is at least ½. The hypothesis says it is exactly ½, the smallest value possible.

Why it matters

In step 6, the wave of a zero grows like xβ. The error ψ(x) − x is the sum of all these waves. Since every β = ½, every wave grows like √x, and the total error stays near √x (up to a logarithmic factor). If even one zero had β = 0.8, the error would, again and again, reach sizes close to x0.8. The same holds for π(x) − li(x).

Key point: the largest real part of the zeros sets how large the error in counting primes can become. The hypothesis says it is ½, the best possible value, and it has now been proven.
