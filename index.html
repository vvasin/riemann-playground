The Riemann hypothesis, step by step

The Riemann hypothesis is a claim about where one particular function, the zeta function, is equal to zero. It was stated in 1859 and is still unproven. It matters because the zeros of this function control how regularly the prime numbers are spread among the whole numbers.

This page builds the idea from the beginning in nine steps. You need arithmetic, powers, sums, complex numbers, and sine and cosine. Each step has a small calculation you can change. Every number and graph on the page is computed from the actual formulas.

Step 1 of 9

Prime numbers and the counting function π(x)

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that is divisible only by 1 and by itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …

Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from the order of the factors. For example, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3, and no other set of primes multiplies to 12. This fact is used in step 4.

Primes become rarer as numbers get larger, and they do not follow a simple pattern. There are 25 primes up to 100, but only 16 between 1001 and 1100. After the prime 1129, the next prime is 1151.

To study how primes are spread out, we count them. π(x), read “pi of x”, is the number of primes less than or equal to x. For example, π(10) = 4, because the primes up to 10 are 2, 3, 5 and 7. Here π is only the name of this function. It has nothing to do with the number 3.14159…

The question behind this page: how accurately can π(x) be predicted without listing the primes?

Step 2 of 9

Estimating how many primes there are

Around 1800, Gauss noticed from tables of primes that near a number n, roughly one number in every ln n is prime. (ln is the natural logarithm.) Near 1000, ln n ≈ 6.9, so about one number in 7 is prime. Near 1 000 000, about one in 14 is.

If a number n is prime with “probability” about 1/ln n, then the expected number of primes up to x is about

1/ln 2 + 1/ln 3 + 1/ln 4 + … + 1/ln x

A smooth version of this sum is called the logarithmic integral, li(x). (Precisely, li(x) is the area under the curve 1/ln t from t = 0 to t = x.) A simpler but less accurate estimate is x / ln x.

The prime number theorem, proved in 1896, says that the ratio π(x) / li(x) gets closer and closer to 1 as x grows. So the estimate is right in relative terms.

The difference li(x) − π(x), however, keeps growing. How fast it grows is the open question. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a precise answer: about as fast as √x, up to a factor of ln x (step 9).

Key point: the difference between π(x) and li(x) is the error of the estimate. The rest of the page explains what controls this error.

Step 3 of 9

The zeta function

Pick a number s. Raise every whole number to the power s, take the reciprocals, and add them all:

ζ(s) = 1/1s + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …

This is the zeta function. ζ is the Greek letter zeta.

For s = 2 the sum is 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + … The terms shrink quickly enough that the total approaches a fixed number, 1.644934… In 1734 Euler showed that this number is exactly π²/6, where π is the usual circle constant.

For s = 1 the sum is 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + … The terms still shrink, but too slowly: the total grows without limit. The sum has a finite value exactly when s > 1.

So far s is an ordinary real number. In step 5 it becomes a complex number.

Key point: ζ(s) is defined by adding 1/ns over all whole numbers n. This sum works for s > 1.

Step 4 of 9

Euler's product: how zeta is tied to the primes

Take s = 2. For each prime p, write a sum that uses only the powers of p:

for 2: 1 + 1/2² + 1/4² + 1/8² + …
for 3: 1 + 1/3² + 1/9² + 1/27² + …
for 5: 1 + 1/5² + 1/25² + …
and so on for every prime

Now multiply all of these sums together. To expand such a product, you pick one term from each sum and multiply the picks. For example, pick 1/4² from the first sum, 1/3² from the second, and 1 from all others. The result is 1/(4·3)² = 1/12².

Since 12 = 2² × 3 in only one way (step 1), the term 1/12² appears exactly once in the expansion. The same holds for every whole number n: 1/n² appears exactly once. So the product of all these sums is 1 + 1/2² + 1/3² + 1/4² + …, which is ζ(2).

Each of the sums is a geometric series: 1 + q + q² + … = 1/(1 − q) when −1 < q < 1. Here q = 1/p², so the sum for the prime p equals 1/(1 − 1/p²). The same argument works for any s > 1:

ζ(s) = 1/(1 − 1/2s) × 1/(1 − 1/3s) × 1/(1 − 1/5s) × 1/(1 − 1/7s) × …One factor for each prime. Valid for s > 1.

This formula, found by Euler, also shows that there are infinitely many primes. As s approaches 1, the left side grows without limit (step 3). A product of finitely many factors would stay finite, so the number of factors, and therefore of primes, must be infinite.

Key point: the sum over all whole numbers equals a product over the primes. This is why the zeta function carries information about primes.

Step 5 of 9

Complex inputs and the zeros of zeta

In 1859 Riemann allowed s to be a complex number, s = σ + it. σ (sigma) is the real part and t is the imaginary part.

What 1/ns means for complex s

Write n = eln n and use Euler's formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ. Then

1/nσ+it = (1/nσ) · (cos(t ln n) − i sin(t ln n))

So each term is a complex number of size 1/nσ, turned by the angle −t·ln n. The size depends only on σ. The sum still has a finite value when σ > 1, and does not when σ ≤ 1.

Extending zeta beyond the sum

Something similar happens with the geometric series. The sum 1 + q + q² + … works only for −1 < q < 1, but the formula 1/(1 − q) gives a value for every q except 1. For zeta, one such formula uses the alternating sum

η(s) = 1 − 1/2s + 1/3s − 1/4s + …Because the signs alternate, this sum has a finite value for every σ > 0.

For σ > 1 one can check that ζ(s) = η(s) / (1 − 2/2s). The right side also makes sense for 0 < σ ≤ 1 (except at s = 1), and that is how ζ is defined there. It has been proven that there is only one way to extend ζ that keeps it smooth (complex differentiable), so every correct formula gives the same values. A different formula, the functional equation, extends ζ to σ ≤ 0.

Why ζ(s) = η(s) / (1 − 2/2s)
ζ(s) − η(s) = 2·(1/2s + 1/4s + 1/6s + …)
           = (2/2s) · (1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + …) = (2/2s) · ζ(s)
so η(s) = (1 − 2/2s) · ζ(s).The even terms appear with + in ζ and − in η, so subtracting leaves twice the even terms. Since 1/(2k)s = (1/2s)(1/ks), the even terms add up to (1/2s)·ζ(s).

Zeros

A zero of ζ is an input s with ζ(s) = 0. There are two kinds:

  • Trivial zeros at s = −2, −4, −6, … They come from the functional equation, are fully understood, and play no part in what follows.
  • Nontrivial zeros, which all lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1, called the critical strip. The first one is at s = ½ + 14.1347…i.

A nontrivial zero is usually written ρ = β + iγ (rho, beta, gamma). They come in mirror images: if β + iγ is a zero, then so are β − iγ, (1 − β) + iγ and (1 − β) − iγ. The panel shows only the upper half (t > 0).

Key point: ζ can be evaluated at complex numbers. Its nontrivial zeros are points β + iγ in the critical strip. Every one found so far has β = ½.

Step 6 of 9

One zero produces one wave

To connect the zeros with primes we use a counting function that is a little different from π(x). ψ(x), read “psi of x”, is a staircase that steps up at every prime and every power of a prime. The step at a power of p has height ln p:

x:    2    3    4=2²  5    7    8=2³  9=3² …
step: ln2 ln3 ln2    ln5 ln7 ln2    ln3   …

The step heights ln p compensate for primes becoming rarer (about one in ln n, step 2), so ψ(x) stays close to x. ψ contains the same information as π: either can be computed from the other. It is used because its formula is simpler.

Riemann (1859) and von Mangoldt (1895) proved an exact formula for ψ:

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − ln(2π) − ½·ln(1 − 1/x²)The sum Σ runs over all nontrivial zeros ρ. Valid for x > 1. At a step, the formula gives the midpoint of the step.

Except for the sum over zeros, the right side is a smooth curve. So every step of the staircase is produced by the zeros.

Why each zero gives a wave

For a zero ρ = β + iγ, Euler's formula gives

xρ = xβ · xiγ = xβ · (cos(γ ln x) + i sin(γ ln x))

Adding the term of the mirror zero β − iγ cancels the imaginary parts. Together the pair contributes the real function

−2·xβ · (β cos(γ ln x) + γ sin(γ ln x)) / (β² + γ²)

This is a wave. Its two numbers play different roles:

  • The height γ sets how fast it oscillates. The oscillation is regular in ln x, not in x.
  • The real part β sets how fast its size grows: in proportion to xβ. For β = ½ the size grows like √x.
Key point: each pair of zeros adds one wave to the prime staircase. The height of the zero gives the frequency; the real part gives the growth rate.

Step 7 of 9

Adding the waves rebuilds the prime staircase

Start with the smooth part, x − ln(2π) − ½·ln(1 − 1/x²), and add the waves of the first N pairs of zeros. The panel uses the first 50 pairs, with heights γ from 14.13 to 143.11.

With N = 0 the curve is a nearly straight ramp. As N grows, the waves add up so that the curve rises steeply at primes and prime powers and stays nearly flat in between. With all infinitely many zeros, the formula gives the staircase exactly.

The lower graph shows the same thing number by number. For each whole number n, it shows how much the curve rises between n − 0.45 and n + 0.45, next to the actual step of ψ at n. With 50 pairs, every prime and prime power up to 30 stands out, and the other numbers stay close to 0. Note that 25 = 5² and 27 = 3³ produce steps too: the waves locate prime powers, not only primes.

This is not a practical way to find primes. To resolve the steps at larger x you need more and more zeros, and testing a number for primality directly is much easier. The formula matters for theory: it shows that the positions of the zeros determine how the primes are spread out.

Key point: the prime staircase equals a smooth ramp plus one wave for each pair of zeros.

Step 8 of 9

The Riemann hypothesis

Every nontrivial zero of the zeta function has real part ½.

In the picture of step 5: all nontrivial zeros lie on the vertical line σ = ½, called the critical line.

What is known

  • All nontrivial zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. That none lie on the edge σ = 1 is what proves the prime number theorem (1896).
  • Infinitely many zeros lie on the line (Hardy, 1914), and more than 40% of all of them do (Conrey, 1989).
  • The first 10 trillion zeros have been computed, and all are on the line.
  • No proof is known. Checking any finite number of zeros cannot settle a statement about infinitely many. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute.

Why ½

Zeros come in mirror pairs with real parts β and 1 − β (step 5). If one zero has β > ½, its partner has β < ½, and vice versa. So the largest real part among all zeros is at least ½. The hypothesis says it is exactly ½, the smallest value possible.

Why it matters

In step 6, the wave of a zero grows like xβ. The error ψ(x) − x is the sum of all these waves. If every β = ½, every wave grows like √x, and the total error stays near √x (up to a logarithmic factor). If even one zero had β = 0.8, the error would, again and again, reach sizes close to x0.8. The same holds for π(x) − li(x).

Key point: the largest real part of the zeros sets how large the error in counting primes can become. The hypothesis says it is ½, the best possible value.

Step 9 of 9

What a proof would guarantee

Von Koch (1901) showed that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a statement about counting primes. Schoenfeld (1976) gave it in a form with explicit numbers:

If the Riemann hypothesis is true, then for every x ≥ 2657:
|π(x) − li(x)| < √x · ln x / (8π)Conversely, if this inequality holds for every x ≥ 2657, the Riemann hypothesis is true. Here π is again the circle constant.

So the hypothesis is exactly a statement about how far the count of primes can drift from the estimate li(x) of step 2. For comparison, the best bounds proven without the hypothesis are much weaker. They are smaller than x only by a factor that grows more slowly than any power of x, such as √x or x0.01.

The panel shows real values. In each case the actual error is well below the bound. For x up to about 1026 the inequality has in fact already been proven, because the zeros it depends on have been checked by computer. The hypothesis would make it true for every x.

Other consequences

  • Gaps between primes: there would always be a prime between x and x + C·√x·ln x for a fixed constant C (Cramér, 1920).
  • Many existing theorems: a large number of results in number theory have been proven under the assumption that the hypothesis holds. A proof would make all of them unconditional.

What it would not give

  • A formula that produces the primes, or the n-th prime, directly.
  • A fast method for factoring large numbers. It would not break encryption such as RSA.
  • An answer to whether a particular number is prime. That is done by primality tests.
Summary: the zeta function is a sum over all whole numbers (step 3) and also a product over primes (step 4). Its zeros (step 5) each add one wave to the prime staircase (steps 6 and 7). The size of these waves depends on the real parts of the zeros. The Riemann hypothesis says all these real parts are ½ (step 8), which is the same as saying the prime count never drifts from li(x) by more than about √x · ln x.

About the calculations. The heights γ of the first 50 nontrivial zeros are taken from published tables. ζ(σ + it) is computed with Euler–Maclaurin summation. li(x) is computed with Ramanujan's series. ψ(x) and π(x) up to 100 000 are computed exactly; π(10k) for k ≥ 6 are published values. The formula in steps 6 and 7 is the explicit formula for ψ, cut off after N pairs of zeros; the cut-off is the only source of difference from the exact staircase. The zero with β ≠ ½ in step 8 is hypothetical.
