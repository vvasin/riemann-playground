The Riemann hypothesis, step by step

The Riemann hypothesis is a claim about where one particular function, the zeta function, is equal to zero. It was stated in 1859. In November 2026 a proof was released and checked by computer (step 9). It matters because the zeros of this function control how regularly the prime numbers are spread among the whole numbers.

This page builds the idea from the beginning in ten steps. You need arithmetic, powers, sums, complex numbers, and sine and cosine. Each step has a small calculation you can change. Every number and graph on the page is computed from the actual formulas.

About the calculations. The heights γ of the first 50 nontrivial zeros are taken from published tables. ζ(σ + it) is computed with Euler–Maclaurin summation. li(x) is computed with Ramanujan's series. ψ(x) and π(x) up to 100 000 are computed exactly; π(10k) for k ≥ 6 are published values. The formula in steps 7 and 8 is the explicit formula for ψ, cut off after N pairs of zeros; the cut-off is the only source of difference from the exact staircase. The zero with β ≠ ½ in step 9 is hypothetical.
