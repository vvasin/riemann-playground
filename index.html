01 / 07We need a mystery first

What makes primes special?

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that you can divide evenly only by 1 and itself.

2, 3, 5, and 7 are prime. But 6 isn't: it's 2 × 3. Every whole number greater than 1 is built from primes, like words are built from letters.

They don't appear at equal distances. Sometimes they are close. Sometimes there are long gaps. The question is: can we predict how they are distributed?

New tool: π(x), read “pi of x”, simply means “how many primes are at or below x?” It is unrelated to the circle constant π.

02 / 07The unexpected connection

Meet a sum that secretly contains every prime.

Imagine taking every positive whole number, squaring it, turning it upside down, and adding the results:

1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + ···

That is the Riemann zeta function at input 2, written ζ(2). At other inputs, you change the power: 2 becomes s.

Here's the twist. The same infinite sum can be made by multiplying one special factor for each prime. That works because every integer has one unique prime factorization.

Key discovery: A formula that mentions all integers can be rewritten using only primes. That is the bridge between zeta and prime numbers.
Next: So far we have only put the ordinary number 2 into zeta. What happens when mathematicians allow stranger inputs?

A finite product cannot equal the infinite result exactly. ζ(2) = π²/6, where this time π really is the circle constant.

Why does multiplication work?

The factor for 2 expands to 1 + 1/2² + 1/2⁴ + ···. The factor for 3 expands to 1 + 1/3² + 1/3⁴ + ···. Multiplying factors lets you pick a power of each prime. Because every integer has a unique prime factorization, you get each term 1/n² exactly once.

03 / 07Now the weird part

What is a zero of zeta?

For an ordinary function, a zero is an input that makes its output equal 0. For example, f(3) = 3 − 3 = 0, so 3 is a zero of f.

Zeta is more mysterious. Mathematicians extend it beyond the original infinite sum and allow complex inputs: numbers with a real part and an imaginary part. Think of them as addresses on a flat map: s = a + bi.

At certain addresses, zeta's complex output is exactly zero. These addresses are its zeros. A few easy zeros are −2, −4, −6, …; the interesting ones are called nontrivial zeros.

Why care? These unusual zero locations determine oscillating correction terms that appear in an exact formula connected to primes. We'll build that formula shortly.

04 / 07The million-dollar question

Riemann's bet: they all sit on one line.

All nontrivial zeros lie in a vertical region called the critical strip, between real parts 0 and 1.

Riemann proposed something much stronger: every nontrivial zero has real part exactly ½. This is the Riemann Hypothesis, or RH.

We've checked enormous numbers of zeros, and the ones checked lie on the line. But there are infinitely many. Checking any finite number, even trillions, is not a proof.

Why does that half matter? In the formula we will use, a zero with real part β contributes a wave whose envelope scales like xβ. If β were larger than ½, that particular contribution could grow faster.

RH is about control: every nontrivial zero on one line means none of these zero terms has a faster xβ power than x½.

05 / 07What a proof would actually buy us

Not a prime-number cheat code. A powerful guarantee.

We already know that primes become less common in a fairly predictable way. A smooth curve called Li(x) gives a very good approximation to the number of primes below x.

But the actual count wobbles around the prediction. RH would put a strong limit on how large those wobbles can be.

Specifically, a famous equivalent statement is that π(x) − Li(x) = O(√x log x). In plain English: beyond some scale, the counting error cannot grow faster than a constant times √x log x.

That strengthens a vast range of results in number theory, including bounds on how far you need to look to find primes. It would not immediately give a magic formula for the next prime, a fast way to factor enormous numbers, or a proof that all encryption is broken.

What would be proven? A precise global limit on the irregularity of prime numbers—not a list of every prime number.

06 / 07Here is the central trick

Build the prime-number staircase out of waves.

Instead of counting primes directly, mathematicians often use a close relative, ψ(x) (“psi of x”). It makes a jump at each prime power: 2, 3, 4 = 2², 5, 7, 8 = 2³, 9, …

Why this strange staircase? Because ψ(x) has an astonishing exact formula using zeta's zeros. It starts with a smooth ramp, and then each zero adds an oscillating correction.

07 / 07Try it yourself, then test the result

Can the waves help us find 23?

Let's zoom in on 20 through 25. For each whole number n, we'll measure how much our reconstructed ψ(x) rises between n − 0.45 and n + 0.45.

In the exact staircase, the jump is ln(p) at a prime power pk and 0 otherwise. With only 50 zero pairs, the estimated jump is fuzzy. It often gives a striking signal at 23, but there can be false positives and false negatives.

We then perform a completely separate, exact divisibility check to confirm whether the candidate is prime. This distinction matters: assuming RH is true does not make a 50-wave approximation an exact primality test.

If RH were proved, a serious search could proceed like this: RH gives rigorous large-scale bounds on prime-counting errors and on how large gaps between primes can get (on the order of √x · log(x), with constants and conditions). Those bounds help choose how far to search. Zeta zeros can suggest where a rise may occur; a primality test then identifies the actual prime.

Crucially, the zeros-to-primes formula does not depend on RH. We can use the 50 known zeros right now. RH would tell us where all the unseen zeros must lie and would sharpen the guarantees.

Full circle: primes → zeta's prime product → zeros → waves → approximate prime-power locations → exact primality check.
Final takeaway: A proof of RH would tighten our understanding of prime-number distribution. The remarkable connection between zeta's zeros and primes already exists as a theorem, whether RH is true or false.
